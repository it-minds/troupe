// WHAT IS INSIDE WHAT

Four layers

The model is the one part we did not write, and it sits innermost.

So what

Everything an organisation cares about is decided outside the model: what an agent may touch, who said yes, what it cost, what was logged. Those live in the harness, which is why the model can be swapped by one line of configuration without any guarantee changing. The harness's edge is one protocol, and our own apps get no private door through it: what the console can do, a script can do, and an audit from either is the same audit.

What it rules out

A vendor lock at the model layer, and a client that is more trusted than another.

// THE SAME HARNESS, TWO HOMES

Where the work runs

The same harness runs on a laptop and in a pod. What changes is who sets the rules.

So what

A developer can try everything alone with the daemon, then move the same kind of session to the cluster without learning a second product; the terminal, the desktop app and any program written against the protocol work in both places. The difference between the two homes is authority, not features. At home the person's own configuration decides which calls ask first. On a pod the profile decides, and a repository's files cannot loosen it.

What it rules out

An agent that writes a file or runs a command without the rules having been consulted first, in either home.

// ASK ONCE, THEN TALK DIRECTLY

The plane and the pods

The plane decides who and where. It is never in the data path.

So what

A client asks the plane for a session, gets a pod and a short-lived token, and from then on talks to the pod. Session content never passes through the plane, so the plane keeps the index, the budget and the audit of administration, and nothing that could leak a transcript. It holds no key either: signing is done for it by OpenBao. Compromising the internet-facing plane yields requests that still have to pass policy, not transcripts and not a cluster. A laptop needs no plane at all.

What it rules out

A central component that can read every session, and a single hop whose failure stalls every running turn.

// FOUR SOCKETS

Bring your own

Four sockets, no favourite piece in any of them.

So what

Adopting Troupe does not mean choosing a model vendor, a tool vendor or a hosting answer. The model socket takes Anthropic, OpenAI or the gateway you already run; the tools socket takes the team's MCP servers and your own; the client socket takes the terminal, the desktop app or something you wrote; the last socket takes a laptop or your cluster with your sign-in. An organisation keeps the opinions it already has.

What it rules out

A feature that only works with one model, one client or one host.

// THE NOUNS

Actors, sessions, profiles

Every event names who did it. A session is its log. A profile is what a team is held to.

So what

Accountability needs no second system. "Who approved this shell command" is a line in the session's log, hash-chained to the one before it, and the same line a client, a restart and an auditor all read. Because a session is nothing but its log, going dormant, waking on another pod, being reviewed months later and being erased are all operations on one record. Because a profile is one object, an organisation says in one place where a team's sessions run and with what; the money is the team's, not the profile's.

What it rules out

A transcript that differs between what the person saw and what the audit shows.

// THINK, ACT, REPEAT

One turn

A turn is one input and everything the agent does with it. The gates sit inside the loop.

So what

A "yes" is specific: to one call, on one turn, recorded with who gave it. The agent runs several tool calls at once where it can, but a call that needs a person waits for that person and nothing runs on their behalf meanwhile. Every delta, call and answer is a line in the log, so a client that attaches late sees exactly what one that was there saw. Budgets end a turn and ask; they do not make it vanish.

What it rules out

A blanket approval that covers calls nobody has seen yet.

// WHAT RUNS WHERE

On the cluster

One namespace per profile, two kinds of door, and nothing else reachable.

So what

The plane, the operator and the console share the system namespace, with OpenBao beside them. Each profile's pods get a namespace of their own, the Secrets they need injected, and a network policy that admits two things: the plane's internal control channel, and the handful of hosts the profile names. People come in through the plane's HTTPS ingress to ask, and through a worker ingress to talk to their pod directly; a pod reaches out only to the model, its MCP servers, storage and OpenBao. A security reviewer can read one profile file and know what its pods can touch. The blast radius of a compromised pod is one profile's namespace and its listed hosts.

What it rules out

A pod that can reach another team's pods, the cloud's metadata service, or anything a profile did not name.

// THREE JOBS FOR ONE KEY MANAGER

Keys, and erasure

OpenBao holds every key. The plane holds none.

So what

The plane can sign tokens, its version policy and the organisation's document without ever being able to leak the key that signs them. A pod can open only the key slots of the teams it serves, one slot per session, so a pod naming a session it does not hold gets nothing. A person's own slots, for private sessions and their own tool credentials, are reachable by nobody else, not by a pod and not by an administrator. Erasure is destroying the key first: once it is gone, the session's bytes are noise wherever they still sit, in the object store, in a backup, on a pod not yet drained. Deleting the rows afterwards is tidiness.

What it rules out

An administrator reading a transcript, and an erasure that depends on every copy being found.

// WORK NOBODY STARTS

Triggers

A trigger is a frozen request, run under terms, by a credential a team owns.

So what

Unattended work is reviewable because nothing about it is loose. What fires is a revision, hashed, so a run is exactly what was approved and not what the file says today, and the session carries that hash in its origin. The terms cap money, turns and time, and set approvals to wait or deny, never to auto. The session runs as a service principal with a subset of the team's grants and no admin role, so a trigger cannot do more than the team could. Every run is flagged for review until a person looks, and a retry with the same key is the same run, never a second one.

What it rules out

A scheduled job that quietly approves its own writes, or escalates past the team that owns it.

// SIX HABITS

Habits of the harness

Six things the harness does without being asked. They are the difference between a demo and something you leave running.

  • Worktrees. A second live session in the same repository gets its own git worktree, so two agents never see each other's half-done edits as yours, and a dirty tree is never removed unasked.
  • The project brief. What the troupe has learned about a repository lives in a file in that repository, written by the agents themselves and read into every prompt. It travels with the code and you can read it too.
  • Watch mode. An AI! comment in a file you save is picked up from whatever editor you use. The harness knows its own writes by content hash and never chases them.
  • Bounded results. A big output is cut where it is made, head and tail with a handle for the rest, and nothing already in the conversation is rewritten, so the model's cache stays warm and the bill stays down.
  • Replay. An agent that dies is rebuilt from its own log and finishes what it started. A finished call never runs twice. The same trick wakes a dormant session.
  • Goal and loop. A goal is seen on every turn and survives a restart. A loop runs turns on its own and stops when the goal is met, at the limit, after three failed turns, or when the budget asks you.
What it rules out

An agent that clobbers your checkout, forgets what it learned last week, or runs up a bill re-reading its own output.

// SCALING FROM DEMAND

It breathes

Pods follow demand. The number of them is never a commit.

So what

Every fifteen seconds the plane counts a profile's active and pending sessions, divides by sessions per pod, adds the warm count, and writes that into the profile's resource; the operator reconciles the StatefulSet to it, and the pods report back. Cost follows use without a person in the loop, inside ceilings a cluster administrator set once. Scaling down drains the pods it removes first: placement stops, running turns finish, sessions go dormant to storage, and only then does the pod go. A profile with no warm workers is zero pods overnight and one pod about half a minute after the first session in the morning. GitOps, where it is used, carries the profile's shape; it never carries a replica count.

What it rules out

A session killed mid-turn by a scale-down, and a pull request to add a pod.

// WHERE CONFIGURATION LIVES

Policy, profile, bundle

Three objects, three owners, three enforcers. Configuration is a fact about the session, not about whose laptop it ran on.

So what

They are easy to blur, so keep them apart. The policy is the cluster administrator's ceiling: which images, which hosts, how many replicas, how much CPU any profile may ask for. It is checked at admission and again by the operator. The profile is the team administrator's choice of where sessions run and with what: image, size, model, storage, egress, the bundle channel it follows. The operator enforces it: the egress becomes a network policy, the secrets are injected rather than written down. The bundle is the content sessions carry, agents, skills and MCP servers, one hashed immutable document per published version. The pod verifies the hash before use and every session is pinned to the version it started on, so an audit can say which agents and tools applied to a turn months later. A new version is for new sessions; retiring one moves them at their next wake, with a logged event.

On a laptop the same bundle sits under your own layers: built-ins, then the bundle, then your config, then the repository's, then the environment, later winning by key. A repository's risky keys count only once you trust the workspace. The faded panel on the right is the usual way, configuration on every laptop, where each machine decides its own model, approvals and tools and nothing pins a run to a version of anything.

What it rules out

"Works on my machine" as an explanation, and a ceiling a laptop can edit out.

// A ROLE IN A FILE

An agent is a file

An agent is a markdown file: a frontmatter contract the harness enforces, and a prose role the model reads.

So what

A role is reviewable like code, versioned like code, and shipped by a team in a bundle like code. The frontmatter says which model role, which tools, which permissions and what share of the budget; the prose says who the agent is. Four layers can hold one, lowest to highest: the harness's built-ins, the profile's bundle, your own configuration, the workspace's .troupe/agents/, and the highest file with the name wins. A session has one root agent, which may delegate and branch, and the same session.create starts it whether a person asked or a trigger fired. On a pod the agent is read from the pinned bundle, so what runs is what was published.

What it rules out

A role that exists only on one engineer's disk, and an agent on a pod that nobody reviewed.

// TOOLS, AND WHOSE THEY ARE

MCP tools

The pod does not need to have your tools. They follow you.

So what

A team's servers come with the bundle and are called under the profile's identity, so everyone granted the profile gets the same view. Your own servers come with you: you attach, you are asked once whether to offer them, the model finds them in the same list as everything else, and a call to one of yours is sent back over your own connection to run on your machine with your sign-in. The token never leaves the laptop, the tools leave when you do, and everyone in the session sees that a laptop is involved. A profile may forbid personal tools on its pods outright, for teams whose pods must take only what the profile names.

What it rules out

Installing a person's credentials on a shared pod, and a tool whose owner cannot be seen.

// WHO IS CALLING

Whose name a call carries

A profile's servers are called as the profile. A person's own servers are called as the person, from the person's machine. There is nothing in between.

So what

The question was sharp because a shared session lets anyone with control steer the agent, and the agent can reach whatever tools are offered. Troupe settled it in two halves. A server the team put in a bundle is called under the profile's own identity, so it answers organisation-wide and who sees what is decided by which teams were granted the profile. A server you signed in to yourself is called back on your laptop, over your socket, and never from the pod; the plane never holds your token. What was rejected is the middle option, the plane exchanging your token on your behalf, because it would make the plane a holder of personal credentials. Around that rule: a profile may forbid personal tools on its pods, everyone in a session sees when one is in play, and a call whose owner left mid-turn waits a minute for them and then fails once, telling the model it may already have run.

What it rules out

The plane holding a person's token, and a personal tool reachable after its owner has gone.

// WHY ERLANG

Why it is built this way

The guarantees above are properties of the runtime, not of code we wrote.

So what

A tool that crashes takes nothing else with it. An agent that dies is rebuilt from the log and finishes. A client that drops, or a slow one, never stalls a turn. Two plane replicas agree on where a session is placed. In most languages each of those is a failure path somebody writes by hand and gets subtly wrong; on the BEAM they are what a supervision tree, a mailbox and a cluster do by default, and have done in telephone exchanges for thirty years. Fewer hand-written failure paths means fewer places for the guarantees to be false. The cost is a runtime fewer people know, and the picture is the one page on this site drawn for the engineers who will: its names are the real ones, and the ledger on the right is the argument.

What it rules out

A bug in one tool taking down a session, and a restart that loses what the agent was doing.
